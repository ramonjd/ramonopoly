It was after having read another 1,500-word pull request description, I realised how close I was to shivving my own eyeball with the pointy end of a USB cable. This wasn't written for me, I concluded. Whoever asked me to review this didn't want my opinion on whether the code was correct or was actually required at all. 

They didn't want me to understand the history, or even the "why", though I suspected, just by reading the tomes of text describing a 30-line code change that I now knew more about the actual change and the reason behind it than the PR author did. 

No.

They wanted me to run their agent's output through my agent, approve with a "Looks Good To Me" and eat a slice of the responsibility pie that no AI agent ever had to taste when its own code broke the internet. 

It wasn't long ago, heck, twelve months ago, that we were writing and coding by hand. Sure a tab completion or two later, but typing `function` at the place you wanted the `function` was the only option. There were more people, there was more time.  

Oh how we considered before pasting! We compared approaches, reached into our toolkits, and spent days arguing over the suitability of variable names. A pull request or three merged was a good day. Above all, we were taught to respect the time of those we invited to review our output, and to reduce the burden of reaching consensus by stating clearly our cases for change. 

This is not a missive against AI. I love it. It helps me chase my ideas faster than I ever could on my own. It helped me fix a leaking toilet. It's going to change our economy and our world. 

Rather, what I realised as I held that USB cable in my trembling hand, was that something had shifted on the creator and consumer see-saw. You see, it's my theory and hope that, as the plank bounces from the earth to the sky and back again, both sides, creator on one side and consumer on the other, do their bit to make it all fun and worthwhile. 

What I'm trying to say with this terrible playground analogy is both playmates accept the cost of that content existing in the first place: the creator pays by means of the time and energy it takes to organise and communicate their ideas. The consumer pays it by digesting, comprehending, empathising with those ideas.

All this is still happening now of course. 

The difference is that one side of that see-saw has become extraordinarily easier to depress. At the same time the other side needs more effort to counter the weight, and spends more of its time in the air trying to do so. 

The time I take parsing the contents of your agent's response, fact-checking its claims with my own agent, and formulating my own response is in inverse proportion to the effort it took to type the originating prompt. In other words, you're fat and I'm mainlining protein powder trying to keep up.

"What am I demanding of my audience?" Probably few people asked this question before the proliferation of AI, but I believe answering that question has become more important than ever now we can write novels with a 5-word prompt. 

Your audience might just be other AI agents, in which case you can relax. Look, as much as we care for our code and the systems that run it, we can spread the consumption cost by creating another AI agent to evaluate and test stuff. At best, the human becomes the judgement filter. At worst, they become a tourist in their own codebase, flicking tokens over their shoulders making wishes to the big Trevi Fountain in the Cloud.

The consumption of literature however, of art, I submit, is not so open to such transactional rebalancing.

We create art because to do so has worth to us.

We consume art because to do so has worth to us.

The rewards of both are enrichment, learning about ourselves, discovering our past and future, a rewiring of our thinking and connection with others. Just for starters.

As audiences, we strive to understand because it is the toll we pay to cross the great expanse, on roads paved by artists, where each stone is a chapter of a book, a stroke of the brush, or a bronze cast. 

This expanse of human invention is still out there, but navigating through it has become more demanding as our internal compasses adjust to the daily task of separating human invention from facsimile. I believe more than ever we need the rough sketches, the streams of consciousness, the voices and the grammatical chaos that can only come from our meaty brothers and sisters. Without all this, humanity's journey to understand itself would not be as interesting.

After all, who is going to read all the poems, the novels, hear the music, or soak in the paintings if not for us? Machines will consume it all in order to complete a task or better respond to our demands of them. 

We will consume them because they are, after all, by us, for us. If we create, not for us, but for the machines, then what is the point.
