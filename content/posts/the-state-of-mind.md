---
title: The state of mind
date: 2026-11-06
---

When writing and coding by hand was the only option, there were more people, there was more time.  

We considered our sentences rather than pasting them, spent days arguing over the suitability of variable names. Such things mattered, or so we thought at the time.

Like any craft, the means of output and language and reason generates a cost for both author and reader: the author pays it to organise the thoughts in their head and communicate them, the reader pays to digest, comprehend, empathise. 

One half of that cost equation has become extraordinarily cheaper. At the same time the other half's has risen. The time I take parsing the contents of your agent's response, fact-checking its claims with my own agent, and formulating my own response is now a mountain against the speck of effort it took to type the originating prompt. 

"Fix this bug". 

"Write a 100-line poem in iambic pentameter."

Hours reduced to seconds.

Content must have an audience, but what are we asking of this audience? If they are human, you may ask them to enjoy some prose. This is not an onerous request. You then ask them to check if the code you committed fixes a critical bug blocking tens of thousands of users. We can reduce the cost equation in the latter situation by creating another agent to evaluate and test the content, the human operator promoted (or relegated depending on how you view it) to a gatekeeper of judgement and value. The former, the consumption of literature, is not so open to such transactional rebalancing. 

We read literature in particular because to do so has worth to us: in enrichment, in learning about ourselves, discovering our past and future, in an effort to rewire our thinking and connect with the numinous melody, plucked on the strings of humanity. We strive to understand because it is the toll we pay to cross the great expanse, on roads paved by artists, where each stone is a chapter of a book, or a cast for bronze.  And the investment in these roads is invariably constituted by many multiples of the fee borne by us when we read a book, or listen to a song.

Who is going to read all the poems, the novels, hear the music, or soak in the paintings? Machines will consume it all in order to complete a task or better respond to our demands of them. Humans will consume them because they are, after all, by us and for us. If we create, not for us, but for the machines, then what is the point.
